# ASYMMETRIC Writing Recipe

Version: 1.0
Last updated: 2026-05-15
For: om-writer — read before every script. This is a directive checklist, not a reference.

---

## Source Channels Analyzed

Wendover Productions, PolyMatter, Johnny Harris, Asianometry, Cleo Abram (Huge If True).

---

## 1. Opening (First 60 Seconds)

**Rule: Arrive mid-story. Something already happened.**

The five channels share one opening principle: the viewer is dropped into pressure, not walked to it. Wendover leads with the most explosive claim first, then works backward. Johnny Harris opens with a visual anchor — a concrete image that proves what the narration hasn't explained yet. PolyMatter states the contradiction outright ("you think X — here's why that's wrong"). Asianometry opens with a constraint the viewer didn't know existed. Cleo Abram opens with the question the video will answer, stated as a fact already in motion.

**Asymmetric directive:**

- Sentence 1: a concrete fact that has already happened, carrying weight. Not a question. Not background. Not a statistic without stakes.
- Sentence 2-3: establish the contradiction or pressure. The system is already under strain.
- By second 30: the viewer knows what is being revealed, even if they don't know how.
- By second 60: one open loop is planted — a question the video will answer, stated obliquely, not spelled out.

**Forbidden in the first 60 seconds:**

- "For years, [industry] has been..."
- Any sentence that could open a Wikipedia article
- Rhetorical questions as openers
- Institutional context before tension

---

## 2. Sentence Density by Section Type

**Mechanism sections — use compression.**

Wendover and Asianometry both tighten sentence length when explaining how the system works. Short declarative sentences. Active voice throughout. No participial phrase openings (wrong: "Having established X, we can now see..." — right: "The mechanism is simpler than it looks.").

**Narrative sections — let it breathe, briefly.**

Johnny Harris and Cleo Abram allow slightly longer sentences when moving through human context, consequence, or stakes. But "breathing" means two or three medium sentences — not a paragraph of throat-clearing.

**Asymmetric directive:**

- Mechanism sections: average sentence under 12 words. Max 18 words. Cut adverbs.
- Narrative sections: average sentence under 18 words. No sentence over 30 words.
- Every section: active voice. No passive in hooks or payoffs. Ever.
- After three short sentences, one longer one can land — for rhythm, not for explanation.

---

## 3. Open Loop Planting and Closing

**The loop is not a question. It is an incomplete claim.**

Wendover uses the inverted pyramid: the conclusion lands first, the mechanism follows. The "loop" is not "I wonder what happens" — it is "you already know the answer but not why it's true." PolyMatter plants the contradiction as the loop ("Singapore is, by definition, a dictatorship — and it works"). Asianometry plants a constraint ("ASML can only make 50 of these per year — and they can't make more") and holds the explanation back.

**Asymmetric directive:**

- Plant the loop as a declarative claim, not a question: "The rate isn't a fee. It's a tax. And the companies paying it have no recourse."
- Close the loop at the payoff, not mid-video. Do not answer the loop the moment you plant it.
- One major loop per video. Sub-loops are allowed in each section but must close before the major loop does.
- A closed loop that delivers no new information is a failed payoff. The closure must land a fact the viewer didn't know.

---

## 4. The Reveal Moment — Sentence Structure

**The leverage point lands as a statement, not a discovery.**

Across all five channels, the reveal does not say "so now you can see that..." — it states the mechanism as if it has always been true and the viewer is late to know it. Asianometry: "Samsung and Intel did not lose TSMC's market. They never had it." Wendover: the airline loyalty program is worth more than the airline itself. These are declarative reversals.

**Asymmetric directive:**

The reveal sentence must:

1. Restate the system in inverted form — the hidden thing is the real thing
2. Be short enough to quote: under 20 words
3. Carry "actually" or "already" or a negation — the reframe is the sentence
4. Be immediately followed by the proof, not more setup

Pattern: `[The thing they thought was X] is [actually Y]. [Here is the proof.]`

Example adapted for Asymmetric: "Apple doesn't charge developers 30 percent for distribution. It charges them for survival."

---

## 5. Payoff — Mental Model, Not Summary

**The last section delivers a framework, not a recap.**

Wendover closes with a reusable model the viewer can apply elsewhere. Asianometry ends with the explicit limits of what was proven ("we don't know what comes next — but the constraint is real"). PolyMatter closes with the implication: now that you understand the mechanism, here is what follows. Johnny Harris closes with consequence for a specific person or group.

None of them summarize. None use "in conclusion" or "as we saw."

**Asymmetric directive:**

- Final section = operator-level takeaway. One reusable principle, stated plainly.
- Pattern: "This is not specific to [system X]. Every [adjacent system] with [same structure] runs the same mechanism."
- Do not restate what the video showed. State what the viewer can now see elsewhere.
- End on the leverage point, not on the subject. The subject is the example. The mechanism is the product.
- Forbidden closers: "so next time you...", "hopefully this helped you understand...", any sentence beginning with "in summary"

## 6. Example Sentence Patterns for Asymmetric Narration

These are patterns — replace bracketed terms with the actual mechanism.

**Opening:**
"In [year], [actor] did something [unexpected]. Nobody covered the part that mattered."

**Mechanism:**
"The [visible thing] is not the [real thing]. The [real thing] is [what controls access / cost / outcome]."

**Constraint reveal:**
"[Actor] cannot [do X] without [paying / asking / routing through] [controlling party]. There is no alternative. That is not an accident."

**Stakes:**
"Every [party in the system] pays this toll. Most of them don't know it has a name."

**Payoff:**
"This is what a [chokepoint / monopoly / extraction layer] looks like from the inside. Once you see the structure, you'll see it everywhere."

---

## 7. Anti-Patterns — What to Avoid

**Anti-pattern 1: The institutional opener.**
"For decades, the airline industry has struggled with..." — background before tension. It belongs on Wikipedia. Cut it. Start after it.

**Anti-pattern 2: Overexplaining the mechanism.**
If narration explains the mechanism for more than 30 seconds without a visual event or clip interrupt, the narration is carrying weight it shouldn't carry alone. Cut or compress. Mechanism explanation must be paced with visual events, not delivered as a verbal block.

**Anti-pattern 3: Personality-forward narration.**
Asymmetric is faceless-first. "I find it fascinating that..." is a brand failure. The system is the subject. The narrator has no face to give.

**Anti-pattern 4: The summary payoff.**
"So what does all of this mean?" followed by a recap is the failure mode. The payoff must introduce something new — the implication, not the summary. If the final section only restates what was shown, cut it and write the reusable model instead.

**Anti-pattern 5: The curiosity question as tension substitute.**
"But why does this happen?" is not tension. It is a postponed explanation. Tension is an incomplete claim that carries weight: "The system was designed this way. It wasn't an oversight." Questions invite the viewer to wait. Incomplete claims make the viewer feel the mechanism is already operating — on them.

## Quick Checklist (Pre-Submit)

- [ ] First sentence: concrete fact, already happened, no questions
- [ ] Open loop planted before second 60 as a declarative incomplete claim
- [ ] Mechanism section sentences: under 12 words average
- [ ] Reveal stated as declarative inversion (under 20 words, immediately followed by proof)
- [ ] Payoff delivers reusable model, not summary
- [ ] No passive voice in hook or payoff
- [ ] No "as we can see" / "in conclusion" / "to summarize" / "so what does this mean"
- [ ] Every line creates tension, reveals mechanism, or lands payoff — or it's cut
